# Precedent case (draft): the City's Waverly commercial revitalization files, 1973-1984

**For:** Pricing the Unpriced team and the sponsor · **Draft of:** 18 October 2026 · **Status:** first draft for review

**Scope.** The sponsor set the scope for this fall in writing: "an indexed summary of the 31 folders plus the interventions table. The before and after comparison can wait." This memo goes with `folder_index.csv` (31 rows) and `interventions.csv` (79 rows).

**Method.**
- Counts come from `archive_scans.csv` as distinct (item, page), not rows.
- Statements about pages come from the one-line captions. Quotation marks are used only where a caption quotes its page.
- No scan image was opened. Open a page's `scan_url` before citing it (AGENTS.md).
- References are *folder-page*: 20-3 is BRG48-43-10-20, page 3. BRG48 is DHCD's record group [W7].

## 1. What the programme was

The Baltimore City Code describes the programme in the City's own words. Baltimore "established the Commercial Revitalization Program": "Selected neighborhood shopping districts were targeted for public improvements". In return, "retail merchants and commercial property owners were required to rehabilitate their properties … in conformance with a unified design developed for the area". The programme "has always been founded on self-help by the business community" (Art. 14 §11-2(d)-(e); Ord. 82-838) [W1].

**How it reached Waverly, according to the captions.**
- **1973.** A Planning paper sets out rehabilitation strategies for Greenmount from 28th to 35th Street (23-245).
- **1975.** The merchants choose consultants Greenfield, Sommer & Associates (23-241). Commissioner Embry names Paul Gilbert coordinator of a new Neighborhood Commercial Revitalization Program. Urban-renewal areas get priority, and a senior architect is assigned to Waverly (23-239, 23-240).
- **1976.** Gilbert describes a $3,000,000 programme for six areas (23-233). A $5,000 planning contract is approved (23-190). A steering committee of merchants and residents meets from 22 March (23-183). Planning surveys shoppers and parking (21-6 to 21-41).
- **1976-79.** Design standards are drafted (folders 5, 23). They are adopted in the Waverly Business Area Urban Renewal Plan, Council Bill 2278 (21-86). About 100 people attend the May 1979 hearing (21-53), and the bill passes by June (21-59).
- **1976-81.** Public works follow:
  - the 33rd Street median, finished May 1979 (19-16);
  - the streetscape, Contract 2572, accepted June 1981 (20-3, 20-8);
  - a rebuilt municipal parking lot, Contract 2611, accepted March 1981 (12-19).

**The deal.** A January 1978 letter asks owners to fix their facades within two years. In exchange the City will build a median, plazas, lights, trees, sidewalks and bus shelters (22-171). In 1979 the City tells an owner of seven properties three things (21-80):
- the standards will bind each property within two years;
- low-interest loans are available;
- the City will spend over $1 million.

**Money stated in the captions.**

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Streetscape, Contract 2572 (final) | $656,415 (20-3) |
| Median contract (also covered York Road) | $98,063 (19-16) |
| Parking lot, all costs (acquisition $149,353) | about $353,390 (24-2) |
| Planning consultant | $5,000 (23-190) |

The total is roughly $1.1 million, close to the Commissioner's "over $1 million" in bond funds (21-84).

**What owners were offered.**
- Free design help from DHCD architects (folders 1-4, 16).
- City REAL loans at 7% over 20 years, and SBA loans (6-3, 16-15).

No caption mentions a facade grant or shows a loan closing. Maryland's Article XI-G (1972, amended 1980) lets the State authorize City loans for commercial buildings [W2]. Whether it is the basis for REAL has not been checked.

**Outside sources.**
- **National Register (2013).** The district has 141 properties [W8]. The nomination credits "a City Council Ordinance that set standards for façade and appearance, and city-financed improvements of parking lots, sidewalks, and benches", citing *The Charles Villager* of October 1981 (NR p. 53) [L1].
- **The renewal plan.** The City still lists a 2012 version of the Waverly Business Area plan [W3]. A 2012 bill added 3313-3319 Greenmount to its acquisition exhibit [W4]. DHCD's 1977 list of available properties also puts 3313 first (26-4).
- **The parking lot.** The 32nd Street Farmers Market began on the rebuilt lot in 1980 [W5; NR p. 40].
- **The streetscape.** The same stretch was rebuilt in 2020-22, again with a brick paver band from 29th to 35th Street [W6].

## 4. How the City worked with merchants

**Merchants were involved from the start.**
- They chose the consultant (23-241) and sat on the 1976 steering committee (5-7).
- The Waverly Development Committee met at the Gorsuch House (26-23).
- The plan sends its own amendments through the merchants' association (8-22).

**The City gave free design help, store by store.**
- DHCD architects Steve Broache and David Hunter drew schemes for Harry's (folder 16), Tugboat Anne's (3-17), the Gorsuch House (1-2 to 1-6) and Laura's Budget Shop (2-3).
- They initialled permits, for example "O.K. S.B." on Shoe World's (2-5).

**The rules came with a grace period and a backstop.**
- Owners had two years to comply. After 24 months' notice the City could condemn property that did not comply (8-13).
- Before the ordinance, staff had no legal basis to stop Burger King using the Boulevard marquee (23-57).
- By June 1980 it was the merchants who asked for enforcement (11-37).

**Merchants changed details.**
- Sign rules were eased after a January 1979 meeting (11-3).
- A tree was deleted when Peddler's Village objected (20-154).
- Work on 33rd Street waited for the Orioles' playoffs (20-180).
- The Christmas shutdown was moved to suit merchants (20-89).
- Driveways were redrawn for individual owners (W020, W055, W056).

**There was friction.**
- Metering the merchants' lot drew a petition of 572 signatures, "some of questionable legitimacy" (21-89, 21-71).
- Merchants were dismayed to learn of the pylon (20-139).
- Funds lapsed in 1978 ("Waverly Loses Allocated Funds", 22-23).
- Construction brought damage complaints (20-24 to 20-29).
- By 1983 the City was writing to merchants "APATHY REIGNS!" (18-2).

**Planning had doubts early.**
- One Planning memo said Waverly is "not Mount Washington or Hampden" (23-69).
- Planning's 1976 study found that no new lots were needed, only better management of the merchants' lot (21-19, 21-41).

## 6. Use for the highest-and-best-use work (careful)

- **Dated use history.** Once the scans are checked, the files give 1976-79 use changes for the curated timeline:
  - the Waverly Theatre, variety store to Shoe World (2-6);
  - a gas station to Tugboat Anne's (3-19);
  - the closed Shell station at Calvin to a Subway (22-178, 22-59; the 3400 address is inferred);
  - a Gulf station to Seven-Eleven (22-73);
  - Burger King in part of the Boulevard (23-55);
  - Eddie's Supermarket closing (22-176).
- **Constraints.** The 1979 plan is still in force as amended, with design controls and acquisition powers [W3, W4, W9]. Section 7 sets out what its current text says.
- **Baselines, not current inputs.** In the 1976 shopper survey, 70% of respondents came from ZIP 21218 and 52% walked in (21-8). The district had 693 parking spaces, with a noon peak of 442 cars (21-23, 21-27).
- **Limits.** The City spent roughly $1.1 million on public works in exchange for private compliance. These captions cannot show whether that changed rents, values or occupancy.
  - Three later signs of trouble are not a before/after test: the 1983 apathy letter, the NR's "high turnover", and the Banner's 2026 count of more than one in four buildings empty (per the reading list).
  - The reading list also notes that Main Street investment tallies are self-reported.
- **Safe use for now: design precedent.** The City paid for shared assets that an assemblage depends on: the lot, the plazas and the street. Facades were left to owners' willingness and capital.

## 7. The plan in force (current text read 18 October 2026)

The Waverly Business Area Urban Renewal Plan was first approved by Ordinance 1029 on 24 May 1979. Bill 2278 in the captions (21-86, 21-59) is the bill behind it. The City publishes a text "revised to include all amendments" through Amendment No. 5, Ordinance 12-710 of 5 December 2012 [W9]. Page numbers below are the plan's own.

**Area.** Both sides of Greenmount from 29th Street (Exeter Hall Avenue on the east side) to 35th Street, with parts of the side streets (pp. 1-4). The whole frontage is inside.

**Uses (pp. 5-6).**
- Community Business permits what the old B-2 zone allowed, "including residential and parking".
- Since Amendment No. 2 (4 December 1995), several uses are not permitted unless they already existed then:
  - pawnshops;
  - liquor and package goods stores;
  - taverns;
  - blood donor centers, massage salons, and poultry and rabbit killing;
  - soup kitchens and check cashing as primary uses.
- Bail bond agencies are not permitted at all.
- **For the highest-and-best-use screen:** the corner saloon is one of the frontage's best-proven historic uses. A new tavern on the frontage is barred by this plan. The screen should treat it as a proven use that is not currently permitted.
- **Check with Planning:** the text cites the zoning code from before the 2017 rewrite (B-2, R-8). Today's parcels carry C-1 (`properties.csv`). How the City applies the use list under the current code needs confirming.

**Rehabilitation standards (pp. 10-18).** These apply to every non-residential property, occupied or vacant.
- *Storefronts.* Show-window elements stay within 13 feet of grade. Solid or enclosed storefronts are not allowed unless they are treated as part of the facade.
- *Materials.* Formstone, aluminum siding, wood shakes and pebble-faced plywood are banned for future use. Existing aluminum siding must be removed. Sandblasting masonry is not allowed.
- *Awnings.* Soft and retractable only, at most 7 feet out and 13 feet up. Rigid awnings and canopies are not allowed.
- *Signs.*
  - Flat signs: area at most three times the frontage in feet, with the top no higher than 13 feet or the second-floor sill.
  - One projecting sign per street-level business: 10 to 15 feet up, at most 4 feet out and 12 square feet a face.
  - No internally lit box signs. Window signs cover at most 20% of a show window.
  - Marquees only on operating theaters. The plan names the Boulevard at 3302 Greenmount.
- *Security grilles.* Metal only, and open during business hours.
- *Compliance and review.* The standards gave owners two years to comply. Any exterior change needs written design approval from DHCD before a permit is issued.

**City powers (pp. 8-9, 21).**
- The City may acquire a property whose owner does not meet the standards within 24 months of written notice. It may also acquire one it judges cannot be rehabilitated.
- If DHCD refuses a demolition permit, it must seek Board of Estimates approval to acquire the property within 90 days. Otherwise it must issue the permit.
- The parcels to be acquired are on a map exhibit whose labels are not in the text layer. Read Exhibit 2 by eye before naming any address.

**Duration and changes (p. 23).**
- The plan runs "not less than twenty (20) years" from approval, and the City still lists it.
- Amendments go to the Waverly Merchants' Association and the Waverly Development Committee for comment before a public hearing.

**What this adds to the case.** The 1979 design deal is still law in substance: the City set the standards, and owners carried the cost. Any assemblage plan on the frontage designs to these rules and avoids the barred uses unless the plan is amended.
